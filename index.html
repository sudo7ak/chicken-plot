1. What the shed actually holds

A Sonali bird finishing at 1.3–1.5 kg needs 1.2–1.5 sq ft of litter floor. Your 1,125 sq ft gives you a real ceiling of roughly 750 birds comfortable, 900 crowded. At 850 you are on 1.32 sq ft a bird, which works only because the jali band runs the full perimeter and gives you cross-ventilation on all four sides.

Foggers change this, and they change it more in Nagpur than they would in a coastal town. Evaporative cooling works on dry heat, and Nagpur's worst weeks — April into early June — are dry, often under 25% humidity. A fogger line down the ridge with the jali open on all four sides can pull 6–8 °C off the shed at 3 pm. That is the difference between 6% mortality and 20%, and it is what makes 850 birds defensible on this floor rather than reckless.

Two things to get right, or the foggers work against you. Run them in cycles, not continuously — wet litter causes more coccidiosis than heat causes death. And they lose most of their effect once the monsoon sets in and humidity climbs past 70%, so your July–September batch still depends on airflow, not cooling. Budget a 1,000 L overhead tank and a 0.5 HP pump, and keep a spare nozzle set: a blocked line on a 45 °C afternoon costs you birds within hours.

4. The helper is your second-biggest cost

At ₹12,000 a month he costs ₹1.44 lakh a year — ₹57,600 a cycle, more than double what the chicks cost. Per bird that is about ₹74, against roughly ₹131 of feed. He is the second line on your cost sheet and he does not shrink when the shed sits empty between batches, which is why the model spreads his salary across all twelve months rather than the four you are growing.

Here is the uncomfortable arithmetic. Feeding, watering and cleaning after 850 birds is two to three hours of work a day. You are paying a full-time wage for part-time work, because 1,125 sq ft cannot hold enough birds to absorb a full-time salary. Three ways to fix it, in order of how well they work:

  • Give him the selling, not just the feeding. If he runs the gate sale and the delivery drops that move you from ₹190 to ₹300/kg, he generates about ₹1.16 lakh a cycle and pays for himself four times over. This is the only version where the salary is clearly a good decision.
  • Stagger two half-batches three or four weeks apart. Same shed, same helper, but you always have birds ready to sell — which is also what makes weekly direct selling possible.
  • Part-time or shared. Six to seven thousand a month for a morning-and-evening routine, with you covering the rest, roughly halves the burden while the operation is still finding its feet.

Whichever you choose, keep him on the same batch from brooding to sale. Brooding is where most mortality is decided, and a helper who has seen a batch through learns to spot a sick bird days before a new one would.

6. The ₹300 question

₹300/kg is what a customer pays at a counter for a live gavran bird. It is not what a trader hands you at your gate for 780 birds at once. Across Maharashtra, gavran and country birds trade between ₹170 and ₹250/kg, with Sonali quoted at ₹300 in Mumbai, ₹250 around Aurangabad and ₹200 from Pune farms. Nagpur runs below Mumbai. So ₹300 is achievable — but as a selling price you charge, not a price you are offered.

The whole plan turns on which of these you actually operate. At 782 birds of 1.35 kg, that is 1,056 kg a cycle:

The third is the easiest to start with and it is what the mixed row models.

Set the live-rate slider to 190 and then to 300 and watch the annual figure. That swing is your business plan.

7. Adding eggs

Sonali is dual-purpose — a hen lays 185–200 eggs a year — so eggs are genuinely on the table. But a layer occupies the shed for eighteen months instead of four, and you have 1,125 sq ft. Eggs and meat are not additive here. They compete for the same floor.

Desi eggs trade around ₹8.50 wholesale against ₹5.84 for commercial eggs in Maharashtra, and retail ₹8–15 depending on where you sell. That premium is the same perception premium that gets you ₹300/kg for the bird, and it comes from the same customers.

What eggs actually buy you

The argument for a small layer flock is not margin. It is these three things.

  • Cash every week instead of twice a year. 150 hens at ₹12 an egg net roughly ₹8,500 a month, which covers most of the helper's salary and the feed bill between bird sales. On a meat-only farm you spend four months paying out before anything comes back.
  • Eggs build the customer list that makes ₹300/kg possible. This is the real argument. A household that comes to your gate every week for a dozen desi eggs is exactly the household that will buy a bird at ₹300. Eggs are how you get 45 people in the habit of coming to you — and that habit is the thing standing between the ₹190 row and the ₹300 row in the previous section.
  • Two prices instead of one. When the live rate softens, or Shravan takes meat buyers off the market for a month, eggs keep selling.

If you do it

9. The Nagpur calendar

Two constraints shape your placement dates, and they pull in opposite directions.

Heat. April to mid-June is the danger window. Birds in their last month during those weeks eat less, gain less and die more. A batch that broods in April is fine; a batch that finishes in May is not.

Demand. Shravan takes a large share of Maharashtra's meat buyers off the market for a month, and Navratri does it again. Selling into either is selling into a soft rate. Winter months and the run-up to Diwali are your strong windows.

BatchPlaceSellRead
1Mid-SeptemberLate December / JanuaryBest batch of the year. Cool grow-out, strong winter rate.
2Late JanuaryEarly MayFinishes into heat. Cut to 700 birds, run foggers, sell at 100 days rather than 120.
3Late JuneMid-OctoberMonsoon brooding needs dry litter discipline. Time the sale after Navratri, into Diwali.

Three batches is achievable but leaves only two to three weeks of clean-out between them. If a batch runs long or a disease scare forces a longer rest, drop to two and take the better rate instead.

10. What kills batches

  • Heat stress. Highest-probability, highest-cost risk on this site. Foggers, ridge ventilation, cool water, and feeding at night in May.
  • Coccidiosis and CRD. Wet litter is the trigger. Fix leaking drinkers the same day. Keep litter under 25% moisture — if it clumps in your fist, it's too wet.
  • Ranikhet and Gumboro. Non-negotiable vaccination schedule; get the exact one from the local Animal Husbandry office, since the challenge pattern is district-specific.
  • Rate collapse at sale. You cannot store a live bird. Every extra week costs feed. Line up two buyers, not one.
  • Feed price spike. Maize drives it. Buying a cycle's feed at placement fixes your biggest cost against a four-month window.
  • Dogs, mongoose, rats. The 3 ft cement band plus buried mesh at the wall foot is your defence. This is why the wall matters more than it looks.

11. Before you place chicks

  • Confirm the buyer and the rate in writing, even informally. Everything above depends on this.
  • Gram Panchayat NOC for the poultry activity, and a check on local distance rules from habitation.
  • Registration with the District Animal Husbandry office; ask about state backyard-poultry and NABARD-linked subsidy schemes, which change year to year.
  • FSSAI registration if you will dress and sell meat yourself. It is inexpensive and it is what lets you charge retail rates.
  • A named hatchery for Sonali chicks with a delivery date, and a second one as backup.
  • Bore or municipal water tested for salinity. Bad water quietly wrecks growth.

1. What the bird is

Sonali is a cross of a Rhode Island Red male with a Fayoumi female, developed in Bangladesh in 1986 and now common across eastern and central India. It looks and eats like a desi bird — multicoloured plumage, lean tight-fibred meat, deep yellow-yolked eggs — which is exactly why it sells at gavran rates rather than broiler rates.

It is dual-purpose: hens lay 185–200 eggs a year if kept. Adult males reach 1.8–2.0 kg, females 1.2–1.8 kg. For your purpose the bird is a meat bird sold at 1.2–1.5 kg, well before it matures.

Three properties matter for a Nagpur shed. It tolerates tropical heat better than a broiler. It forages and takes coarse feed, so a portion of the ration can be kitchen grain, greens or bran. And it is hardy — it will forgive management mistakes that would wipe out a broiler batch, which makes it a sensible first breed.

The trade-off is speed. A broiler hits 2 kg in 40 days; Sonali needs 100 days for 1.2 kg. You are not competing on efficiency. You are competing on what the meat sells for.

2. Brooding — the first 21 days

Most of your mortality is decided in these three weeks, and most of it comes down to temperature. Chicks cannot regulate their own body heat until about day 14. Too cold and they pile and smother; too hot and they dehydrate and stop eating.

Weigh 20 chicks on day 7. If the average is under 100 g, something is wrong with heat, water or feed access, and you still have time to fix it. That single weighing is the cheapest diagnostic you will ever run.

3. Equipment per 850 birds

Feeder and drinker counts matter more than they look. Under-provide and the timid birds at the bottom of the pecking order never get enough, which shows up as an uneven batch — a spread of weights at sale rather than a tight one, and the small birds are pure loss.

4. Feed programme

Feed ad lib to week 6, then twice a day — early morning and late evening. In April and May shift the bulk of feeding to night, because a bird will not eat at 44 °C and you lose a week of growth to heat alone.

From week 8 Sonali will take up to 20–25% of its ration as coarse material without losing much growth: broken maize, rice bran, kitchen greens, azolla. On 850 birds that is a real saving on your largest cost. Change the ration gradually over 4–5 days — an abrupt switch causes a growth check and loose droppings.

Handling rules that decide whether any of this works. Live vaccines stay at 2–8 °C until the moment of use — carry them in a cooler with ice packs, never in a pocket. Reconstitute and use within two hours. For water-route vaccines, withdraw water for 1–2 hours first so every bird drinks, use skim milk powder at 2 g/litre to protect the virus, and never use chlorinated water or a metal vessel. 8. Litter and water

Litter is the most under-managed thing on a small farm and it causes more losses than any single disease. Paddy husk or groundnut shell, 3–4 inches, stirred every second day so it does not cake. The test is your fist: squeeze a handful, and if it clumps and holds shape it is too wet and coccidiosis is coming. If it powders and stings your eyes with ammonia, it is too dry and dusty and CRD is coming.

Fix leaking drinkers the day you notice them. Sprinkle lime under the drinker line if damp starts. Remove and replace caked patches rather than turning them in.

A bird drinks roughly twice what it eats, so about 3.5 kg of water per bird over the cycle — around 3,000 litres for your batch. That doubles in a Nagpur May. Scrub drinkers daily; a biofilm in a drinker undoes a vaccination programme.

9. Biosecurity

  • All-in, all-out. One age group in the shed at a time. Mixing ages is how a farm becomes permanently infected.
  • Rest between batches. Two to three weeks after cleaning, washing and lime-washing. This is not idle time, it is a disease break.
  • Foot dip at the door, refreshed every two days. Most disease arrives on footwear.
  • No visitors into the shed, especially anyone who keeps birds or works at another farm. Let buyers see birds at the gate.
  • Buried mesh at the wall foot. Rats carry salmonella and eat feed; your 3 ft cement band handles dogs and mongoose.
  • Dead birds burned or buried deep the same day, away from the shed and any water source.

10. Keeping Sonali for eggs

Sonali is dual-purpose, so the same bird you sell at 1.35 kg will lay 185–200 brown eggs a year if you keep her instead. Egg weight runs 50–55 g and the shell is the tinted brown that buyers read as desi. A hen comes into lay around day 150–160 and is worth keeping for about thirteen months of production.

The commercial layer is the trap. On volume and feed efficiency it wins easily, at 1.8–2 kg of feed per dozen against roughly 2.6 for a coloured bird. But it earns around ₹220 a hen a year at wholesale rates, which is a business at five thousand birds and pointless at a hundred and fifty. It also leaves you a spent hen worth almost nothing, where a Sonali is still worth about ₹320.

Getting the birds

Buy female chicks or point-of-lay pullets, never straight-run. Half a straight-run batch is cockerels, and you would feed them for five months before finding out they do not lay. Rearing your own female chicks costs roughly ₹290 a pullet but means five months with no income; buying at point of lay costs ₹350–450 and has you selling eggs within a month. For a first flock, buy point-of-lay — it separates learning to sell eggs from learning to rear pullets.

Housing the layers

ItemWhat it needs

Floor eggs are the commonest failure with a new flock, and they are mostly a nest-box problem: too few boxes, too bright, too exposed, or introduced too late. Have the boxes in place two weeks before the first egg so hens learn them before they need them.

Feeding a layer

Switch to layer feed at 18 weeks, or when the first eggs appear — whichever comes first. A layer eats about 110 g a day, roughly 3.3 kg a month, and the critical difference from grower feed is calcium: layer ration carries 3.5–4% against under 1% in grower. Feeding grower feed to a laying hen gives thin shells first and bone problems later.

Offer shell grit or limestone chips free-choice in a separate pot. Hens self-regulate calcium, and they need most of it in the afternoon because the shell forms overnight — an afternoon top-up of grit does more for shell quality than raising calcium in the main ration.

Water matters more for a layer than a meat bird. An egg is 70% water, and a hen deprived of water for a few hours in summer will stop laying for several days. Two drinker refills a day, scrubbed, and never let the tank run dry in May.

Handling the eggs

  • Collect twice a day, three times in summer. An egg left in the litter through a hot afternoon is a cracked or soiled egg, and a dirty desi egg does not fetch a premium price.
  • Do not wash eggs. The shell has a natural cuticle that keeps bacteria out; washing removes it and shortens shelf life. Wipe a soiled egg dry with a cloth, or sell it separately.
  • Store pointed end down in a cool, shaded place. Fresh unwashed eggs keep well for 2–3 weeks, but your customers are paying for freshness — sell within a week.
  • Grade by size. Early-lay eggs are small and pullet eggs at 40 g should not go out at the same price as 55 g eggs. Consistency is what lets you hold ₹12.
  • Keep them away from onions, kerosene and paint. Shells are porous and eggs pick up smells.

The vaccination schedule in section 6 covers a bird sold at four months. A hen kept eighteen months needs more: an R2B booster at 16–18 weeks, and a Lasota repeat every 8–10 weeks through the laying period, since Ranikhet in a layer flock shows up as a collapse in production before it shows up as dead birds. When to cull

Production falls below about 45–50% after roughly seventy weeks, and at that point the hen is eating the same 3.3 kg a month for a third fewer eggs. Sell her then. A spent Sonali hen at 1.6–1.8 kg still fetches a proper price as a desi bird — unlike a commercial layer, which is why the eighteen-month cycle works financially. Replace the flock in one batch rather than birds one at a time, so you keep a single age group in the pen.

11. The daily routine

WhenTask
Early morningWalk the shed before feeding. Look at how birds are standing and spread, not just whether they are alive. Feed, refill and scrub drinkers, remove any dead bird.
MiddayCheck temperature and airflow. In summer, foggers on, and check the tank has not run down.
EveningSecond feed, stir the litter, quick head count of anything sick or separated from the flock.
WeeklyWeigh 20 birds and write it down. Top up litter. Check the fogger nozzles.
Every batchRecord chicks in, deaths by week, feed bags used, weights, sale weight and rate. Four batches of this and you will know your own farm better than any guide can tell you.

The weekly weighing is the one habit that separates farms that improve from farms that repeat the same batch for years. It tells you when growth stalls, which is the earliest sign of nearly every problem here — days before you would see a sick bird.

Vaccination ages follow standard Indian schedules for coloured and backyard birds. Confirm the specific programme with the District Animal Husbandry office in Nagpur, and use a veterinarian for any antibiotic or dosing decision — nothing here is a substitute for someone who can examine the flock.
